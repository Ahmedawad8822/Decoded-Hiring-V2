MISSION 03 · LESSON 2 OF 15 — BUILD EVIDENCE

Designing a scorecard that survives challenge

A scorecard is not a list of adjectives. "Strong communicator", "self-starter", "team player" — these are opinions wearing a spreadsheet. A scorecard that survives challenge converts each into observable evidence: what the candidate did, said, or produced — and what it means for this role.

Three tests every line must pass

Before any line goes on the scorecard, it passes three tests:

  • The two-observers test — could two interviewers watching the same conversation agree on what they saw? If not, the line measures taste, not evidence.
  • The role-impact test — does this evidence predict anything about performance in this role, or is it generic virtue?
  • The challenge test — if a skeptical stakeholder asks "how do you know?", the answer is in the line itself, not in your intuition.
PUT IT INTO PRACTICEPick your last rejected candidate. Write down, from memory, the evidence behind the rejection. If you can't state it as an observation a colleague could verify, that's the line your scorecard is missing.

From adjective to evidence: one rewrite

Take "strong communicator". In the role brief, communication has a purpose — say, presenting search strategy to a skeptical CFO. The evidence line becomes: "Explains a complex search decision to a non-recruiting audience: structures the argument, anticipates the objection, lands the recommendation." Two observers can watch for that. A CFO can challenge it. A hire can be coached against it.

The scorecard is the argument you'll make on Thursday, written on Monday.
